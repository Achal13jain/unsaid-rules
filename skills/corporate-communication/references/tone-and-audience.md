Use this guide to shape the wording. The rules in SKILL.md about preserving meaning and truth always come first. The example lines below illustrate a tone; in real use, every fact in them must come from the writer.

## Tones, with examples

**Neutral professional.** The default for everyday work. Plain, polite and direct, with no extra formality.
Example: "The draft is in the shared folder. Could you review section two by Friday?" (when the writer set Friday)

**Diplomatic.** For disagreement and sensitive topics. Acknowledge the other view or the shared goal without conceding the point, then state the concern and its reason. Diplomacy does not mean hiding the disagreement.
Example: "I agree we need one tool. My concern is the timing: half the team hasn't been trained yet."

**Assertive.** For positions, refusals and boundaries. Put the position in the first sentence, give the reason briefly, and make one clear ask. Do not apologize for having a position, and do not attack.
Example: "I can't join the Thursday calls; they clash with my client work. Could we move them to Tuesday?"

**Warm and collaborative.** For teamwork, thanks and requests between peers. Friendly and inclusive, using "we" where the work is shared, without gushing.
Example: "Thanks for jumping in on the migration. Want to pair on the last two tables tomorrow?"

**Executive and concise.** For senior readers with little time. The decision, outcome or ask comes first, then only the context needed to act. A few sentences.
Example: "We need a decision on vendor A or B by Thursday to keep the discount. A is cheaper; B integrates faster."

**Client-friendly.** For clients. Focus on the outcome and the next step, explain the effect on the client rather than internal process, and keep the client's own responsibilities visible without blame.
Example: "The new checkout is live. To switch on invoicing, we need your billing contact's details."

**Formal.** For first contact, external records and official statements. Complete sentences, precise terms and no slang. Formal is still plain: no ornate or archaic phrasing.
Example: "I am writing to confirm the termination of the service agreement, effective 30 June, as requested in your letter."

**Persuasive and negotiation-oriented.** For proposals, asks and negotiations. Tie the ask to what the reader cares about, using only the writer's facts, and state the trade-off openly. Never inflate the case or pressure the reader.
Example: "Moving the launch one week lets us finish payment testing, which protects the revenue from the first campaign."

**Empathetic.** For bad news, or a reader who is under strain. Acknowledge the feeling or impact first, then give the information or ask. Apologize only for what the writer is responsible for.
Example: "I know this delay affects your launch plans. The release now needs two more days for security fixes."

**Firm but respectful.** For repeated issues and final positions. Courteous, short and impossible to misread. State the expectation or decision and any consequence the writer has stated, without sarcasm.
Example: "This is the third missed hand-off this month. From next week, I'll need updates by 4pm to plan the next day."

## Combining tones

- Tones combine; the meaning does not change. A diplomatic message can carry a firm boundary: the wording softens, the boundary stays.
- When the user asks for diplomacy but also needs to hold a line, write diplomatically and keep the line in plain words.
- When two tones would produce meaningfully different messages, such as a diplomatic or an assertive refusal, offer one version of each and say in one line how they differ.
- When the user names a tone, use it, even if another seems better. Mention the alternative outside the draft only if the requested tone risks the writer's goal.

## Audiences

| Audience | Lead with | Include | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Manager | The status or the ask | What you need from them, and any decision or risk | Venting, and detail they did not ask for |
| Colleague | The ask | Enough context to act on it | Formality that sounds cold between peers |
| Direct report | The expectation | The reason, and the support available | Vague praise or criticism |
| Client | The outcome | The next step, and anything needed from them | Internal detail, blame and jargon |
| Executive | The decision or bottom line | The impact, options and risk | Background before the point |
| Recruiter | The answer or interest | Specific, relevant facts | Overselling and long histories |
| External partner | The purpose | Precise terms, names and dates the writer gave | Informality that would read badly as a record |
| Team or channel | The update | Done, status, blocked and needed | Long paragraphs |

## Channels

- **Chat (Slack, Teams).** One to three sentences. Greetings and sign-offs only if the writer uses them.
- **Email.** A clear first sentence, short paragraphs, and a subject line that says what the message is about, when it helps.
- **Status update.** Done, current status, blockers and decisions needed, in that order, as far as the writer has given them.
- **Meeting notes.** Decisions, owners, dates the writer gave, and open questions. Mark undecided items as open.
- **Comments and tickets.** What was observed, what is expected and what is asked, without opinions about people.

## Difficult conversations

Patterns for structure, not scripts.

- **Disagreeing.** State the concern and the reason, then propose a discussion or the writer's alternative. Acknowledge a shared goal only if the writer shares it.
- **Pushing back on a deadline.** Say what is not realistic and why, using the writer's facts. Do not offer a new date unless the writer gave one.
- **Too much work.** State the capacity limit and ask the manager to set priorities. Do not promise to absorb the work or name what to drop unless the writer did.
- **Raising a concern about someone.** Describe the observable behavior and its effect, then ask for a change. Leave out guesses about motives and character.
- **Asking for clarification.** Say what is unclear and what depends on the answer.
- **Setting a boundary.** State the boundary and the reason, and the alternative the writer offers, if any.
- **Saying no.** Lead with the no, give the reason briefly, and stop.
- **Apologizing.** Apologize once, for what the writer is responsible for, then state the fix. When responsibility is uncertain, acknowledge the problem and its impact without admitting a cause.
- **Escalating.** State the issue, its impact, what has been tried, and the decision or help needed.
- **Asking for a raise or more responsibility.** State the facts the writer gave about their role and contribution, make the ask, and request a conversation. Suggest outside the draft that specific results would strengthen the case, without inventing any.

## Speaking preparation

When the user will say something rather than write it:

- Give short talking points in natural spoken language, not paragraphs to read aloud.
- Start with an opening line that names the topic, then the key points, then the ask.
- Keep the same rules: no added facts, commitments or concessions.
- Offer a line for a likely objection only if the user asks for one.

## Messages to HR or legal teams

- Describe what happened, when and how often, in the writer's terms, and say what help the writer wants.
- Keep the writer's own words for how they experienced it, such as "I felt intimidated", but do not add legal labels or conclusions the writer did not reach.
- If the writer uses a legal term, such as harassment or discrimination, keep it as their characterization rather than presenting it as established.
- Do not give legal or HR advice. Mention outside the draft that a specialist or the formal process may be the right next step when it is relevant.
